# OTPPhone Demand Validation Playbook

**Prepared:** 5 June 2026
**Purpose:** Turn the demand-validation action item from the research findings into an executable plan. It sets out what to test, who runs it, how to interview, exactly which organisations and events give access to each buyer segment, how to read the signals, and the decision gate that follows.
**Style of evidence:** Independent and behavioural. The aim is evidence an outside investor would accept, not reassurance from friends.

> One reframing before anything else. The instinct is to ask "is there demand for OTPPhone?". The more useful question is "which single segment should we build for first, and is that segment's demand real enough to justify the spend?". Treating this as a choice between three segments, rather than a yes or no on all three at once, is the difference between a focused company and a diffuse one.

---

## 1. What we are actually testing

Three claims have to survive contact with real people before further engineering spend is justified:

1. **The pain is real and ranked.** A target user already struggles with secure communications today, and that problem is near the top of their list, not a "nice to have".
2. **Our wedge beats the incumbent for that user.** For their specific job, OTPPhone is meaningfully better than Signal, a hardened messenger, or whatever they use now, in a way they can articulate without being prompted.
3. **Someone with a budget will commit.** A person who can authorise money or adoption will take a concrete step (a letter of intent, a paid pilot, a fleet trial, a documented endorsement), not merely express enthusiasm.

If all three hold for at least one segment, you have a validated beachhead. If only the first two hold, you have an interesting product and no business yet. The plan is designed to separate those two outcomes deliberately.

## 2. Who runs this, and why it is not you alone

A founder asking "would you use my product?" gets polite answers. The findings and Cameron both made the same point: this validation cannot be done credibly by the technical founder alone, for two reasons. The first is bias, because people soften criticism when they are talking to the person who built the thing. The second is access and credibility, because defence and government doors open to people with the right background and network, and close to a cold approach.

Recommended setup:

- **Recruit a commercial partner to lead interviews.** A business-development or marketing collaborator, ideally with some defence, government or NGO exposure. Cameron's suggestion of a marketing, sales or business student at Plymouth is a reasonable low-cost start, provided the work is scoped and supervised rather than handed off.
- **Keep the founder in a listening seat.** You attend to hear the problem in the user's own words, not to pitch. A useful discipline is that the founder is not allowed to describe OTPPhone until the interviewee has finished describing their world.
- **Split roles by segment.** The journalist and NGO segment can be run by the commercial partner directly. Defence and government almost always need a warm introducer, so identify who that is for each approach before you start (Cameron's army contact, a RUSI member, a prime's small-business team).

## 3. Method and rules of evidence

The method throughout is structured customer discovery in the style of Rob Fitzpatrick's "The Mom Test". The single rule that matters: ask about the interviewee's past behaviour and real constraints, never about their opinion of your idea.

Operating rules:

- **Behaviour over opinion.** "What did you use on your last sensitive assignment, and what went wrong?" is evidence. "Would you buy a one-time-pad phone?" is noise.
- **Separate the four roles.** User, buyer, budget-holder and influencer are rarely the same person, especially in defence and government. Record which one you are speaking to and never assume a keen user implies a willing buyer.
- **Do not lead.** Avoid naming one-time pads, metadata resistance or any feature until the person has described their own needs unprompted. If they reach for your differentiator on their own, that is a strong signal. If you have to introduce it, it is weak.
- **Pre-register kill criteria.** Before interviewing, write down the result that would make you stop or pivot (see section 9). Deciding the threshold in advance prevents you from rationalising weak data later.
- **Capture consistently.** With consent, record or take structured notes against the same template every time, then code each interview for problem severity, current solution, switching triggers and any commitment offered. Consistent capture is what lets you compare across a segment.
- **Opsec and ethics.** You are asking people about how they protect themselves, which is sensitive in itself. Do not record without explicit consent, anonymise notes, never press anyone to disclose operational detail, and accept that some people cannot talk to you at all. Do not raise expectations you cannot meet.

A practical target is six to twelve good interviews per segment before you draw any conclusion, with "good" meaning a real member of the target population, not a proxy. Patterns usually stabilise around eight to ten.

## 4. Segment A: Journalists and NGOs (run this first)

**Why first.** It is the fastest, cheapest and most honest signal. These users are reachable year-round through known organisations, they will tell you plainly what works and what does not, and their threat models (spyware, device seizure, source protection) overlap heavily with the harder segments. Treat this segment as credibility and distribution rather than revenue, exactly as the findings recommend. The thing you are buying here is proof that the product survives contact with real adversaries and real users, which is precisely the evidence the defence and government buyers will later ask for.

**Access routes (named):**

- **Access Now Digital Security Helpline.** A free, 24/7 service for journalists, activists and human rights defenders, which handled more than 4,000 requests in 2024 and is a member of the civil-society CERT network. The helpline staff and their constituents are an unmatched source of real-world secure-comms pain. Approach the organisation for expert interviews about what users actually need and adopt.
- **Freedom of the Press Foundation.** Runs SecureDrop, used by more than 65 news organisations, and delivers digital-security training to newsrooms. Their trainers see adoption behaviour across the industry.
- **Rory Peck Trust.** Focused on freelancers, who supported more than 430 journalists last year and publishes digital-security guidance for freelancers. Freelancers in hostile environments are your sharpest test users.
- **Internews.** A large media-support NGO and co-author of the Digital First Aid Kit, with field programmes that equip journalists in difficult regions.
- **Wider net for interviews and intros:** Committee to Protect Journalists, Reporters Without Borders, the Citizen Lab (spyware research), the Electronic Frontier Foundation, Tactical Tech, and the Global Investigative Journalism Network.
- **Convenings:** RightsCon is the main digital-rights gathering, although note the 2026 edition was cancelled, so watch for the next one rather than planning around it. Investigative-journalism conferences and regional digital-security trainer meet-ups are useful secondary venues.

**Who exactly to talk to:** working investigative and conflict journalists, freelance fixers, newsroom information-security leads, and the digital-security trainers who advise them. Trainers are high-value because they shape what hundreds of journalists adopt.

**Interview guide (adapt, do not read verbatim):**

- Walk me through the last time you had to protect a sensitive conversation or a source. What did you actually do?
- What are you using now, and what specifically frustrates you about it?
- Tell me about a time your communications security failed or scared you. What happened next?
- Who decides which tools your team or network uses, and how does a new tool get adopted or rejected?
- When you have changed tools in the past, what triggered the switch?
- What would have to be true for you to carry a separate device, rather than an app on your normal phone?

**Signals that count (there is little money here):** unprompted description of a problem OTPPhone solves; a trainer willing to pilot it with a cohort; a newsroom willing to trial it; inclusion in a digital-security curriculum or recommendation list. Adoption and endorsement are the currency, not price.

**Pass or fail for the segment:** at least eight of your target interviewees independently describe a problem your wedge addresses, and at least one credible trainer or newsroom agrees to a pilot. Below that, the journalist thesis is a marketing story, not a beachhead.

## 5. Segment B: Military and defence

**Why second, and slowly.** This is the most valuable and the slowest segment, with multi-year procurement and gated access. Run outreach in parallel with Segment A because the lead times are long, but do not expect quick answers, and do not let the excitement of a colonel's interest be mistaken for a buying signal. The buyer is never the user here.

**Access routes (named):**

- **NATO DIANA.** The Defence Innovation Accelerator for the North Atlantic runs annual Challenge Calls for dual-use deep-tech companies registered in NATO states. The 2026 cohort selected 150 companies across ten challenge areas, two of which ("advanced communication technologies" and "contested electromagnetic environments") map directly onto OTPPhone. Selected firms receive up to 100,000 euros at the first phase and up to 300,000 euros if down-selected, plus access to NATO test centres and, most importantly for validation, structured contact with defence end-users and mentors. The 2026-cohort call closed in mid-2025, so the realistic target is the next Challenge Call, expected in the second half of 2026, via proposals.diana.tech. Even without applying, the challenge statements tell you what NATO buyers say they need.
- **DASA and UK Defence Innovation (UKDI).** The Defence and Security Accelerator, now consolidated into UKDI with around 400 million pounds a year, publishes themed and open calls and provides a route to MOD end-users. Engaging with a relevant call, or simply reading recent ones, is both a funding route and a demand signal.
- **Defence primes' small-business and innovation teams.** Thales, BAE Systems and Leonardo all run supplier and innovation engagement. A prime is a more realistic first customer or channel than a ministry, and their capability people will tell you quickly whether the idea fits a live requirement.
- **RUSI.** The Royal United Services Institute convenes serving and former defence and government people and is a credible route to expert interviews and warm introductions.
- **Cameron's army-comms contact.** The single warmest door you already have. Use it for honest end-user reaction and for the all-network SIM question noted in the findings.
- **Trade events (later milestones, not near-term).** DSEI at ExCeL London is the major UK biennial show, next running 7 to 10 September 2027. The UK Home Office Security and Policing event at Farnborough and NCSC's CYBERUK both ran earlier in 2026 and return in 2027. Because the 2026 editions have passed, near-term defence validation through mid and late 2026 should rely on introductions, accelerator challenge statements and direct approaches, with the 2027 events planned as venues for deeper validation and pilot conversations.

**Who exactly to talk to:** signals and communications officers (the J6 or G6 community), end users in special forces and reconnaissance roles, capability and requirements managers (the people who write what gets bought), and procurement or innovation leads inside primes. Aim to speak to both a user and a requirement-owner for every thread.

**Interview guide:**

- For your role, what communications problem keeps recurring that current kit does not solve well?
- Walk me through how a new communications capability actually gets adopted in your organisation. Who has to say yes?
- What happens today if a device or operator is captured or compromised? Where are the gaps?
- Where does commercial kit (phones, Starlink, off-the-shelf apps) get used in practice, and what worries you about it?
- If a capability like this existed and was accredited, what is the smallest first step your organisation could take, and who would fund it?

**Signals that count:** a named capability sponsor who will champion a trial; a written expression of interest or pilot intent; a fit with a specific accelerator challenge or DASA call; an introduction onward to a requirement-owner. Polite interest with no sponsor and no next step is not a signal, however senior the person.

**Pass or fail for the segment:** at least one identified requirement-owner confirms a real, ranked problem and points to how a pilot could be funded or sponsored, ideally backed by an accelerator challenge fit or a prime's interest. Without a route to a sponsor and a budget, treat military demand as unvalidated regardless of how warm the conversations felt.

## 6. Segment C: Diplomatic and government

**Why last.** Access is the most gated of the three and the precedents (France and Germany) show these decisions are led centrally by security authorities, not bought bottom-up. Validate it after you have evidence from the other two, which also gives you credibility to open these doors.

**Access routes (named):**

- **National security and cyber authorities.** In the UK, the NCSC (part of GCHQ) and its CYBERUK conference are the centre of gravity for government secure-communications thinking. CYBERUK ran in April 2026 and returns in 2027; in the meantime, NCSC's published guidance and its industry-engagement routes indicate priorities.
- **Cabinet Office and Government Digital Service.** The owners of cross-government digital and, increasingly, sovereign-tooling decisions. Hard to reach cold, realistic via RUSI, techUK or a prime.
- **Crown Commercial Service frameworks.** G-Cloud and related frameworks are the mechanical route by which UK government buys software. Studying which frameworks a product like this could sit on tells you how a sale would actually happen.
- **Reference points, not customers.** The teams behind France's Olvid and Tchap (DINUM and ANSSI) and Germany's BwMessenger (BWI) demonstrate what a sovereign buyer specifies. They are already served, so treat them as a way to understand requirements and certification expectations, not as prospects.
- **RUSI and techUK.** Both convene the government and industry people you need and are a credible route to expert interviews.

**Who exactly to talk to:** government chief information security officers and their secure-communications leads, foreign-ministry IT security staff, and the policy people who own digital sovereignty questions.

**Interview guide:**

- How does your organisation decide what is approved for sensitive or classified communications today?
- What drives a move away from a foreign or commercial tool towards a sovereign one?
- What certification or accreditation would a new product need before you could even trial it?
- Where do your current approved tools fall short for your users in the field or abroad?
- If you wanted to pilot something new, what is the smallest unit you could do it in, and who signs off?

**Signals that count:** a named agency confirms a specific gap and a budget line; a clear statement of the accreditation bar (which doubles as a product requirement); a willingness to take a briefing at official level. Anything short of a named problem with a named owner is background interest.

**Pass or fail for the segment:** at least one government security lead confirms a concrete gap, names the accreditation needed, and indicates a realistic pilot unit. Otherwise, government is a long-term ambition rather than a near-term market.

## 7. Sequencing and timeline

A practical first pass is about twelve weeks of focused effort, with defence outreach started early because it is slow.

- **Weeks 1 to 2: set up.** Recruit and brief the commercial partner. Agree the three claims being tested, the interview templates, the capture and coding sheet, and the kill criteria. Draft the target list of named organisations and the warm introducers for each.
- **Weeks 2 to 6: Segment A in depth.** Run eight to twelve journalist and NGO interviews, prioritising trainers and information-security leads. In parallel, fire the slow-burn defence introductions (Cameron, RUSI, a prime, the next DIANA challenge statements) so they have time to mature.
- **Weeks 5 to 9: Segment B.** Convert defence introductions into interviews with both users and requirement-owners. Map every thread to a possible sponsor and budget route.
- **Weeks 8 to 11: Segment C.** Use evidence from A and B to open a small number of government conversations, focused on the gap and the accreditation bar.
- **Weeks 11 to 12: synthesise and decide.** Score each segment against its pass or fail test and run the decision gate in section 9.

Because the major 2026 UK events have already passed, this near-term pass relies on introductions, accelerator material and direct approaches. Pencil DSEI 2027 and the 2027 editions of CYBERUK and Security and Policing in as later milestones for deeper validation and pilot discussions, not as gates for this first pass.

## 8. Budget

The dominant cost is people's time, not cash. A realistic first-pass budget:

- **Commercial partner:** the main investment. A part-time contractor or a supervised student for roughly a quarter. Cost ranges from a modest student stipend to a contractor day rate; either way it is small against the engineering spend it protects.
- **Travel and access:** a few hundred to low thousands of pounds for travel to interviews and any relevant smaller events through 2026. The large 2027 trade shows carry their own cost and can be budgeted separately if you reach that stage.
- **Tools:** minimal. A scheduling tool, a transcription tool used with consent, and a shared coding sheet.
- **Accelerator applications:** time rather than money, since DIANA and DASA are non-dilutive.

The point of stating the budget is to make the trade-off obvious: a few thousand pounds and a quarter of focused interviewing is the cheapest insurance available against building the wrong product for the wrong segment.

## 9. Synthesis and the decision gate

Score each segment separately against its three claims (real ranked pain, a wedge the user articulates unprompted, and a committed step from someone with budget or adoption power). Then apply a simple gate:

- **Go (commit to a beachhead).** One segment passes all three claims. Build for that segment first and treat the others as later expansion. The strongest realistic outcome is journalists and NGOs passing on pain and wedge, with one defence thread showing a sponsor and a funding route, which together make a credible "validated beachhead plus expansion" story for investors.
- **Pivot (reposition or re-segment).** Pain and wedge are confirmed but no buyer will commit. Revisit the offer, the price, or which segment you lead with, then re-test the buyer claim only.
- **Stop or rethink.** No segment confirms a ranked pain that your wedge addresses. That is a signal to change the product thesis before spending more on engineering, which is a cheap lesson at this stage and an expensive one later.

What an investor will want to see at the end is not a deck of enthusiasm but a short evidence pack: who you spoke to, what they currently do, the problems they described in their own words, and the concrete commitments you secured. Aim the whole exercise at producing that pack.

## 10. Risks and ethics

- **Confirmation bias is the main threat.** You will want this to succeed. The pre-registered kill criteria and an interviewer who is not the founder are the countermeasures.
- **Warm contacts are finite.** Cameron's introduction and a prime's goodwill can be spent only once. Go in prepared, and do not waste them on early, unstructured conversations.
- **Sensitivity of the subject.** Interviewing people about how they stay safe carries duty of care. Consent, anonymisation and restraint are non-negotiable, especially with journalists and human rights defenders.
- **Do not oversell.** Describing a product that does not yet exist as if it does will cost you credibility with exactly the small community you need.

## 11. First two weeks: a concrete checklist

- Write the one-page brief: the three claims, the kill criteria, and what counts as a committed step in each segment.
- Recruit the commercial partner and agree their scope and supervision.
- Build the interview templates and the shared coding sheet.
- Draft the named target list per segment and identify the warm introducer for each defence and government thread.
- Send the first journalist and NGO outreach, and separately open the slow defence introductions (Cameron, RUSI, a prime, and the next DIANA challenge material).
- Book the first three interviews.
